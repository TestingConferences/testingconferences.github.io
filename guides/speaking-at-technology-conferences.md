title: A Guide to Speaking at Technology Conferences

Speaking at a conference can look mysterious from the audience. In practice, it is a series of learnable steps: find an event, shape an idea, submit it, prepare carefully, and help an audience understand something useful.

This guide follows that journey from the first thought of speaking through the end of the presentation. It is especially written for software testers, quality engineers, and other technology practitioners, but most of the advice applies to any technical community.

> This is an original adaptation inspired by Karl L. Hughes's eight-part CFP Land guide to speaking at technology conferences, which is preserved in the [Internet Archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20220701014119/https://www.cfpland.com/guides/speaking/introduction/). CFP Land is no longer online, so we rebuilt and updated its useful path for today's conference community.

## Part 1: Understand technology conferences

No two conferences are quite alike. Before deciding where to submit, learn what kind of event you are looking at and what its audience expects.

### Topic and depth

Some conferences focus tightly on one practice, tool, or community. A specialist testing event might spend an entire day on exploratory testing, performance engineering, accessibility, security, or test automation. These events let you go deep because much of the audience already shares the vocabulary and context.

Broader technology conferences may cover development, operations, product, design, data, leadership, and quality. A talk at one of these events usually needs a little more context and a clear explanation of why the subject matters beyond your immediate specialty.

### Tracks and formats

A single-track conference puts every attendee in the same room. Your potential audience is larger, but not everyone will have chosen your subject. A multi-track conference lets attendees select the most relevant session, which can produce a smaller but more engaged room.

Events may offer several formats:

- Lightning talks of five to fifteen minutes
- Experience reports or case studies
- Standard sessions of thirty to sixty minutes
- Panels or facilitated discussions
- Hands-on workshops lasting several hours
- Keynotes intended for the whole conference

Choose a format that fits both the idea and your experience. A focused lightning talk is often a better first step than stretching one lesson into a forty-five-minute session. A workshop needs exercises, facilitation, and enough time for attendees to practice; it is not simply a longer talk.

### Audience and scale

An event may be a local meetup with a few dozen people, a regional conference with several hundred, or an international gathering with thousands. Size affects the atmosphere, not necessarily the value. Small events can make conversation and connection easier. Large events may offer more topics and people but can feel less personal.

Look beyond attendance numbers. Ask who comes to the event, what experience levels it serves, and whether attendees are mostly individual contributors, leaders, consultants, academics, tool builders, or a mixture.

### Organizers and incentives

Conferences can be community-run, nonprofit, commercial, or organized by a vendor. Each model can produce an excellent event, but its goals influence the program. A vendor event may favor its ecosystem. A volunteer event may have limited money and staff. A commercial conference may have stronger production support while expecting content that helps sell tickets.

Read the code of conduct, speaker agreement, refund policy, and sponsor information. Find out whether speakers receive travel, lodging, an honorarium, a free ticket, or no financial support. None of these arrangements is automatically right or wrong, but you should understand the exchange before committing.

### Why attend before speaking?

Attending helps you learn how a community talks about its problems. You can discover techniques, meet collaborators, find future employers or employees, and see different presentation styles. You will also notice what makes an event welcoming—or difficult—to navigate.

You do not have to attend conferences before you are qualified to speak. Your practical experience can be valuable now. Attending simply gives you more context for choosing the right event and designing a useful session.

## Part 2: Decide why you want to speak

Conference speaking takes time. A clear reason will help you choose opportunities, respond to setbacks, and decide when to say no.

### Share a lesson

You do not need to be the world's leading expert. You need a useful experience that you can explain honestly. A failed automation rewrite, an accessibility bug that changed your process, or a team experiment that improved feedback time may be exactly what another practitioner needs.

The strongest experience reports include boundaries. Explain the context, what you tried, what happened, and where the lesson may not apply. Audiences usually trust a specific account more than a claim that one practice solves every problem.

### Learn more deeply

Preparing to teach exposes gaps in your understanding. Questions from reviewers, peers, and attendees force you to define terms, test assumptions, and support claims. Even when you already know the subject, turning experience into a coherent explanation can make you better at the work.

### Build relationships

Speaking gives people a reason to start a conversation with you. Those conversations can lead to collaborators, mentors, jobs, podcast invitations, open-source contributions, or friendships. Treat these as possible outcomes, not debts the audience owes you.

### Represent a perspective

Conference programs improve when they include people from different roles, regions, industries, career stages, and lived experiences. You may be able to describe constraints or communities that are rarely represented on stage.

Do not wait until you feel like a universal authority. Be precise about what you know, credit the people who helped, and share the view you can responsibly offer.

### Grow a career or business

Speaking can demonstrate communication skills and subject knowledge. It may support consulting, training, developer relations, leadership, or a job search. If your employer benefits, discuss whether speaking preparation counts as work and whether the company will cover travel.

Keep promotion proportional. A useful talk may mention a relevant product or service, but a disguised sales pitch breaks trust and is rejected by many conferences.

### Make the commitment realistic

Decide how much time and money you can spend, how far you can travel, and how public you want to be. Consider accessibility, caregiving, visas, health, and employer approval early. Virtual, local, and internal events are legitimate speaking opportunities and can fit constraints that destination conferences do not.

Write down a modest first goal, such as giving one ten-minute talk, submitting to three well-matched events, or developing one session you can improve over a year.

## Part 3: Find calls for proposals

A call for proposals—often shortened to CFP—is the period when a conference invites people to suggest sessions. It may also be called a call for papers, call for participation, or call for speakers.

### Start with conferences you understand

Look at events you have attended, communities you participate in, and conferences listed on [TestingConferences.org](/). Follow organizers and local groups, subscribe to event newsletters, and watch the official event site for dates.

Conference directories and CFP newsletters help with discovery, but always verify the details on the organizer's official page. Deadlines, formats, themes, and event dates change.

### Read the whole call

Before drafting, record:

- The submission deadline and time zone
- Event dates and location
- Tracks, themes, and intended audience
- Accepted session formats and lengths
- Required proposal fields and word limits
- Whether review is anonymous
- Whether previously delivered or published material is allowed
- Speaker benefits and expected costs
- Code of conduct and speaker terms
- When decisions are expected

Do not assume every CFP uses the same definition of beginner, workshop, case study, or keynote. Use the language in the call.

### Check the fit

Review the current theme, previous programs, and videos when available. This shows the level and style of content the event selects. It can also reveal that your idea was covered recently, giving you a chance to choose a different angle.

Fit is not the same as imitation. The program needs sessions that belong at the event, but it also needs ideas it has not already presented repeatedly.

### Track opportunities

A small spreadsheet or task board is enough. Include the conference, proposal, deadline, status, decision date, event date, costs, and important links. Set reminders before the deadline so you have time for feedback and technical problems.

Start selectively. Three thoughtful submissions to suitable events teach you more than twenty generic submissions sent everywhere.

### Watch for warning signs

Be cautious when an event hides fees until late, pressures speakers to buy tickets, lacks identifiable organizers, has no code of conduct, copies another conference's material, or sends unsolicited acceptance for a subject you never proposed. Ask organizers direct questions. Declining a questionable opportunity is part of managing a speaking career.

## Part 4: Write and submit a proposal

A proposal is not the talk itself. It is evidence that you understand the audience, have a focused idea, and can deliver the session described.

### Begin with the audience outcome

Complete this sentence: “After this session, attendees will be able to…” Use a concrete verb. Compare approaches, diagnose a problem, design an experiment, or apply a technique is clearer than understand or learn about.

Then define the audience. A session for test automation beginners should not quietly require advanced knowledge of distributed systems. A leadership talk should explain why its lesson matters to people responsible for teams or strategy.

### Narrow the idea

A useful talk usually has one central promise supported by a few points. “Everything about software testing” is too broad. “Three ways our team made flaky end-to-end tests easier to diagnose” establishes a problem, scope, and likely outcome.

Narrowing does not make an idea less impressive. It makes the proposal credible and the talk memorable.

### Write a clear title

The title should help reviewers and attendees predict the subject. Personality and wordplay can help, but clarity comes first. If a clever title could describe five unrelated talks, add a subtitle or rewrite it.

Avoid claims the talk cannot support. Words such as always, never, perfect, and effortless invite skepticism and rarely reflect real engineering work.

### Build the abstract

Unless the CFP requests a different structure, a strong public abstract answers four questions:

1. What problem or opportunity does this session address?
2. Why does it matter to this audience?
3. What will the speaker cover or demonstrate?
4. What can attendees take back to their work?

Write for a person scanning many submissions. Open with the substance, use plain language, and remove background that does not help someone choose the session.

### Give reviewers the detail they need

Many systems include private notes, an outline, or a field for the program committee. Use it. Describe the session's progression, examples, evidence, demonstrations, exercises, and timing. Explain what is original about your perspective and disclose vendor relationships.

If the talk relies on a case study, include enough context to evaluate it. If it makes an empirical claim, identify the evidence. If it is interactive, describe how the interaction will work for the expected room and time.

### Write an appropriate biography

Your biography should establish why you can give this particular session. Mention relevant work, community experience, or prior exploration without turning it into a complete career history. First-time speakers can demonstrate credibility through the work itself; prior conference appearances are not the only qualification.

### Get feedback and revise

Ask at least one person in the intended audience and one person unfamiliar with the topic to read the proposal. Useful questions include:

- What do you think this talk will teach?
- Who is it for?
- Which sentence is confusing?
- What would make you attend—or skip—it?
- Does the promised outcome fit the session length?

Read the abstract aloud, check every required field, preserve a copy, and submit before the last hour. Confirm that the system shows the proposal as received.

## Part 5: Understand how conferences choose talks

Selection is usually program design, not a ranking of speakers from best to worst. A strong proposal can be declined because it duplicates another session, misses the theme, targets the wrong level, or does not fit the available schedule.

### Eligibility and initial review

Organizers may first remove incomplete, late, commercial, off-topic, or code-of-conduct-incompatible submissions. Following the instructions is the easiest selection factor to control.

Reviewers then assess some combination of relevance, clarity, usefulness, originality, evidence, audience fit, and feasibility. In anonymous review, they may not see your name or biography at this stage.

### Program balance

The committee has to assemble a whole program. It may need a mix of beginner and advanced sessions, technical and human topics, talk formats, industries, and perspectives. Ten excellent proposals about the same popular tool cannot all occupy one track.

Organizers may also consider speaker availability, travel budget, room capacity, previous program repetition, and whether a session depends on equipment they cannot support.

### What you can influence

You cannot control the other submissions or the program's constraints. You can make the proposal easy to evaluate:

- Match the stated audience and theme
- Describe a focused problem and outcome
- Show a realistic plan for the time available
- Support claims and avoid hype
- Explain what distinguishes the session
- Follow every submission instruction

Do not try to reverse-engineer a secret formula. Review processes vary, and a technique that worked at one event may be irrelevant at another.

### Improve through reviewing

If a community offers open proposal mentoring or volunteer reviewing, participate when you are qualified and have the time. Reading proposals teaches you how quickly ambiguity, excessive scope, and missing outcomes become visible. Respect confidentiality and declare conflicts of interest.

## Part 6: Handle acceptance, waitlists, and rejection

Every submission ends in one of a few outcomes. Responding professionally protects both you and the organizers and makes the next step easier.

### If the proposal is accepted

Celebrate, then verify the commitment before accepting. Confirm:

- The exact event date, session length, and format
- Whether the title or abstract may be edited
- Travel, lodging, visa, ticket, and payment arrangements
- Recording and licensing terms
- Accessibility or technical requirements
- Important rehearsal and material deadlines

Tell the organizers quickly if you cannot meet a condition. It is better to decline promptly than to accept and hope a major conflict disappears.

Once confirmed, block preparation and travel time. Save the organizer's contact details somewhere accessible without internet service.

### If the proposal is waitlisted

A waitlist is a real possibility, not a promise. Ask when a final decision is likely and whether you should reserve travel. Do not make nonrefundable purchases unless the organizers clearly agree to cover them.

Keep preparing only to the degree that the likelihood and timing justify. If another commitment creates a conflict, update the organizers rather than silently remaining unavailable.

### If the proposal is rejected

Rejection is common, including for experienced speakers. It does not establish that the idea is bad or that you do not belong on stage. The program may have received hundreds of proposals for a few dozen spaces.

If feedback is offered, look for specific, actionable themes. Reviewers can be wrong or disagree with one another, so use judgment rather than applying every comment mechanically. If no feedback is offered, do not demand private deliberations from volunteer organizers.

Revisit four possibilities:

1. **Fit:** Was this the right event, track, and audience?
2. **Clarity:** Could a reviewer identify the problem and outcome quickly?
3. **Scope:** Was the plan realistic for the session length?
4. **Evidence:** Did the proposal support its important claims?

Revise the idea, try a shorter version at a meetup, or submit it to a better-matched event. Also allow yourself to retire a proposal. Persistence means learning and choosing again, not sending the same text forever.

### If circumstances change

Withdraw as soon as you know you cannot speak. Give a brief, honest explanation and help the organizer recover time. You do not owe private medical or family details.

Organizers may also cancel or change an event. Keep receipts, understand refund terms, and avoid costs you cannot absorb until arrangements are clear.

## Part 7: Prepare before the conference

Preparation turns an accepted idea into an experience the audience can follow. Start with the promise in the proposal; attendees chose the session based on it.

### Design the story before the slides

Write the central message in one sentence. Choose the few supporting points the audience needs, arrange them in a logical order, and decide what to leave out.

A practical structure is:

1. Establish the situation and why it matters.
2. Show the difficulty, constraint, or failed approach.
3. Explain what changed and why.
4. Demonstrate the result and its limits.
5. Give the audience concrete next steps.

Slides are visual support, not the structure itself. Outline on paper or in plain text first.

### Make evidence understandable

Define necessary terms. Use readable charts, cite sources, and distinguish measurements from opinions. Protect confidential information and remove identifying customer or employee details unless you have explicit permission.

When presenting a success story, include tradeoffs and conditions. When presenting a failure, focus on decisions and learning rather than blaming individuals.

### Plan demonstrations defensively

Live demonstrations are engaging and fragile. Simplify the environment, disable unrelated notifications, enlarge important text, and rehearse the exact path. Prepare screenshots, a recording, or a completed state so the lesson survives if the network or tool fails.

Never expose production credentials, customer data, private source code, or internal messages. Use purpose-built examples.

### Rehearse for time and comprehension

Practice aloud from beginning to end. Silent review does not reveal timing, pronunciation, breath, or awkward transitions. Record a run and watch it once. Ask a peer to identify the main message and any unexplained assumptions.

Rehearse until you can finish comfortably under the limit, including pauses and audience interaction. Decide in advance which optional section to skip if time is short. Do not solve an overlong talk by speaking faster.

### Make the session accessible

Use large text, strong contrast, descriptive link text, and meaningful labels on diagrams. Do not rely on color alone. Describe important visuals aloud and caption prerecorded video. Provide materials in a format that works with assistive technology when possible.

Ask the conference about microphones, ramps, seating, captioning, interpreters, quiet spaces, and other support you need. Accessibility includes the speaker as well as the audience.

### Prepare the logistics

Confirm the slide aspect ratio, connection types, operating system constraints, internet availability, and whether you must provide materials in advance. Bring local copies of slides, fonts, videos, and demo dependencies. Carry the adapters you need and a backup on a separate device or drive.

Know when and where to meet the session chair. Plan travel with enough margin for delays, and keep essential presentation equipment in your carry-on luggage.

## Part 8: Give the presentation

On the day, your job is not to perform perfection. It is to guide the audience through the promised idea.

### Before the session

Arrive early enough to find the room and test the actual equipment. Check that slides, demos, audio, confidence monitor, clicker, and microphone work. Learn how the organizer will signal remaining time and whether questions happen during or after the talk.

Drink water, eat something appropriate for you, and use the restroom. Silence notifications and close unrelated applications. Put a timer where you can see it without repeatedly checking the wall clock.

### Start clearly

Pause, look at the audience, and begin. Briefly establish what the session is about, who it is for, and what people will leave with. You do not need to apologize for being nervous or recite your full biography.

Speak more slowly than nervousness suggests. Use the microphone even if you believe the room can hear you; it may feed recording, captioning, or assistive listening systems.

### Work with the room

Look across different parts of the audience without fixing on one person. Allow time for laughter, reflection, and note-taking. If people enter late or leave, continue; their movement may have nothing to do with your session.

If a demo fails, move to the backup and explain the lesson it was meant to show. If you lose your place, pause, check your notes, and continue from the next point. The audience usually experiences a short pause as much shorter than the speaker does.

### Manage questions

Repeat or summarize an audience question before answering so everyone hears it. Answer the question you understood, and ask for clarification when needed. It is fine to say you do not know, distinguish a hypothesis from a fact, or offer to follow up.

Keep answers useful to the room. If one question becomes a detailed consultation, suggest continuing after the session. Ask the moderator for help if a participant monopolizes time or behaves inappropriately.

### Finish intentionally

Do not race through the conclusion. Restate the central lesson, give the audience a small next action, and show where they can find accessible materials or contact you if you want follow-up.

Thank the audience and organizers, then stop. A clear ending is stronger than adding a new idea after the conclusion.

### After the talk

Save useful questions and feedback while they are fresh. Share slides, code, references, or corrections when promised and when you have permission. Watch the recording if one is published, but review it for one or two improvements rather than cataloging every mannerism.

Thank the people who helped you prepare. Update the talk based on what the audience needed, not only on how the performance felt. Then rest: preparing and delivering a session is real work.

## A simple first-speaker checklist

- Choose one lesson grounded in your experience.
- Identify a specific audience and useful outcome.
- Find a conference whose topic, format, and terms fit.
- Write a clear title, abstract, outline, and biography.
- Ask for feedback and submit before the deadline.
- Treat the decision as program fit, not a verdict on your worth.
- If accepted, confirm the terms and prepare to the proposal's promise.
- Rehearse aloud, make the material accessible, and build backups.
- On the day, help the audience follow one idea at a time.
- Record what you learned and make the next version better.

The path to the stage is not reserved for people who already look like conference speakers. It is open to practitioners willing to share a specific experience with care, clarity, and respect for the audience.
