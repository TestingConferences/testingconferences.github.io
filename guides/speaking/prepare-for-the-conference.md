*Part 8 of 8 in [A Guide to Speaking at Technology Conferences](/guides/speaking-at-technology-conferences/).*

Good preparation removes avoidable uncertainty so you can concentrate on the audience. This article expands on CFP Land's archived [“Preparing for the Conference”](https://web.archive.org/web/20220701014119mp_/https://www.cfpland.com/guides/speaking/pre-conference/).

## One to three months before

- **Get employer approval.** Confirm whether travel and preparation are work time, whether legal or communications review is required, and what information must remain confidential.
- **Reconfirm the event.** Verify dates, venue, session format, reimbursement rules, recording terms, and accessibility arrangements before spending money.
- **Book travel and lodging.** Establish who pays, which expenses qualify, how reimbursement works, and what happens after cancellation. Keep personal extensions separate from covered travel.
- **Prepare documents.** International events may require a passport, visa, invitation letter, insurance, or tax form. Begin early and do not rely on organizers for legal advice.
- **Join speaker communication.** Note briefings, rehearsals, dinners, private channels, and deadlines. These can be valuable places to meet other speakers and resolve common questions.
- **Keep rehearsing.** Lock the main structure early enough to practice the real session rather than endlessly redesigning slides.

## One to two weeks before

Reread the conference website, code of conduct, speaker handbook, and your accepted proposal. Confirm the audience, title, duration, room format, question policy, and material-delivery deadline.

Plan the entire route: airport or station to lodging, lodging to venue, and late-night or accessible alternatives. Review the schedule so you know when to arrive, what overlaps with your talk, and which sessions or people you hope to encounter.

Choose clothing that works for the venue, microphone, lighting, temperature, movement, and your own comfort. Avoid last-minute experiments. Ask what equipment is provided: laptop or bring-your-own device, display connection, aspect ratio, confidence monitor, microphone, remote, power, audio, internet, and dedicated demo network.

Create offline backups of slides, fonts, videos, demos, notes, and contact information. Keep a copy on another device or drive. Disable notifications and ensure examples contain no credentials or private data.

## When you arrive

Find the room and view it from the audience. Locate the stage entrance, lectern, timer, screens, power, accessibility route, and technician. Learn whether the session is recorded and where the camera and captions appear.

Test the actual media and equipment as early as the organizers allow. A successful test at home does not prove that venue audio, adapters, fonts, video, or network access will behave the same way.

Join the event's official community channel if it is useful, but do not let a live social feed derail final preparation. Ask the session chair how you will be introduced, how time signals work, and how questions will be handled.

## In the hours before speaking

Develop a routine that steadies rather than exhausts you. Some speakers walk, stretch, talk with attendees, or listen to music; others need quiet. Eat and drink what works for you, use the restroom, and avoid changing caffeine or medication habits for the occasion.

Arrive in the room before the previous session ends or at the time requested by the organizer. Check slides, demos, audio, clicker, microphone, and timer once more. Close unrelated applications and put devices into a focus mode.

Your job is not to achieve perfection. It is to guide the audience through the useful idea you promised. Pause before beginning, speak more slowly than nerves suggest, and use the microphone because it may also feed recording, captioning, or assistive listening systems.

If a demo fails, move to the backup. If you lose your place, pause and check your notes. During questions, repeat the question for the room and say when you do not know. Finish by restating the central lesson and giving the audience a practical next action.

Afterward, save useful questions while they are fresh, share any materials you promised, thank the people who helped, and give yourself time to recover.

## A simple first-speaker checklist

- Choose one lesson grounded in your experience.
- Identify a specific audience and useful outcome.
- Find a conference whose topic, format, and terms fit.
- Write a clear title, abstract, outline, and biography.
- Ask for feedback and submit before the deadline.
- Treat the decision as program fit, not a verdict on your worth.
- If accepted, confirm the terms and prepare to the proposal's promise.
- Rehearse aloud, make the material accessible, and build backups.
- On the day, help the audience follow one idea at a time.
- Record what you learned and make the next version better.

The path to the stage is not reserved for people who already look like conference speakers. It is open to practitioners willing to share a specific experience with care, clarity, and respect for the audience.
