A call for proposals—often shortened to CFP—is the period when a conference invites people to suggest sessions. It may also be called a call for papers, call for participation, or call for speakers.

## Start with conferences you understand

Look at events you have attended, communities you participate in, and conferences listed on [TestingConferences.org](/). Follow organizers and local groups, subscribe to event newsletters, and watch the official event site for dates.

Conference directories and CFP newsletters help with discovery, but always verify the details on the organizer's official page. Deadlines, formats, themes, and event dates change.

## Read the whole call

Before drafting, record:

- The submission deadline and time zone
- Event dates and location
- Tracks, themes, and intended audience
- Accepted session formats and lengths
- Required proposal fields and word limits
- Whether review is anonymous
- Whether previously delivered or published material is allowed
- Speaker benefits and expected costs
- Code of conduct and speaker terms
- When decisions are expected

Do not assume every CFP uses the same definition of beginner, workshop, case study, or keynote. Use the language in the call.

## Check the fit

Review the current theme, previous programs, and videos when available. This shows the level and style of content the event selects. It can also reveal that your idea was covered recently, giving you a chance to choose a different angle.

Fit is not the same as imitation. The program needs sessions that belong at the event, but it also needs ideas it has not already presented repeatedly.

## Track opportunities

A small spreadsheet or task board is enough. Include the conference, proposal, deadline, status, decision date, event date, costs, and important links. Set reminders before the deadline so you have time for feedback and technical problems.

Start selectively. Three thoughtful submissions to suitable events teach you more than twenty generic submissions sent everywhere.

## Watch for warning signs

Be cautious when an event hides fees until late, pressures speakers to buy tickets, lacks identifiable organizers, has no code of conduct, copies another conference's material, or sends unsolicited acceptance for a subject you never proposed. Ask organizers direct questions. Declining a questionable opportunity is part of managing a speaking career.
